# Copy

Write to sell. Everything here exists because it changed a decision.

## Order of work

**Angle → hook → payoff → body.**

The angle is the frame the whole page is read through. Pick it for what it lets you say later:
a good angle makes the hard parts of the product read as integrity instead of traps.

Never skip to the hook. Most rejected headlines are an angle problem wearing a headline costume.
---

## Before the words

**Whose benefit is it?** Finish "so you can…" without describing the company. If you cannot,
it is a feature. A wider addressable market is a benefit to the seller.

**One audience or several?** Name the reader out loud. If the sentence needs "or", you have
two pages. Two product lines often mean two audiences who never meet, use different platforms
and different words.

**Can the business ship it?** A claim is an operational commitment. Ask who maintains it after
launch. No owner, no claim. The strongest idea in the room is often the one nobody can build.

**Which vocabulary lane?** In regulated or low-trust categories, word choice is a legal
posture. Count the terms across three to five competitors, show the lanes, and get whoever
owns the risk to pick one. Sitting between lanes pays both costs.

**Check every number against the live product first.** Not after the draft. One pass over
the client's own site settles the facts you were about to invent, and it catches the ones
you had already got wrong — a price in the wrong currency, a tier that does not exist, a
split you assumed. Cheaper than a correction and much cheaper than shipping it.

**Test a claim at the edges, not in the middle.** "The same terms on every plan" can read
clean and be false if limits scale with the plan size. A claim that holds for the
typical case and breaks at the extremes is the one that generates refunds, because the
people at the extremes are the ones who checked.
---

## Page spine

Above the fold: hook, payoff, one primary action, proof the reader can check.
Middle: how it works, the product and its price, named proof, the differentiators.
Bottom: objections, FAQ, closing action that restates the offer.

One primary action, repeated. Nothing outranks it above the fold. Secondary links sit visually
below it. A community link or a partner portal above the buy button is a leak.

Cut any block that cannot name its conversion job. Education, company news, language switchers
and event countdowns belong in the footer or nowhere.
---

## The headline

**Sell. Do not defend.** Handling the objection in the hero tells the reader there is something
to defend. Objections go where resistance happens, after the price.

**Sell. Do not gatekeep.** A demanding angle belongs in the tone and in how the rules are
framed. A line that says the reader might not qualify loses them at peak interest.

**One idea.** Three claims in one line means none of them land.

**No price. No spec sheet.** Numbers sell hard in the payoff. In the headline they are a tell
that the ideas ran out.

**Check the syntax is not owned.** Go past claims to sentence shape. A structure a rival
already runs makes you the second firm doing their move.

**Watch the hygiene factors.** In a low-trust category the obvious differentiator is table
stakes. "We pay." "Our rules are clear." Claiming those sounds defensive and invites the
comparison you least want.

**Length:** measure it from the brand's headlines that worked, and from the format's
character limits. Put the brand and the promise in it when you can.
---

## Buttons

**A button names the next click, not the outcome.** Nobody gets the outcome by clicking. The
only honest promise a button can make is what the next screen shows, and specific
low-commitment labels out-click aspirational ones when the headline already carries the
promise.

**One verb, object changing with depth**, beats one fixed string: pick your plan → pick
your size → start. Each one names the decision actually in front of the reader.

**An outcome label can still win** when it is the category's search term and the client
accepts the collision. Say the collision out loud once, then build what they chose.
---

## The payoff

Explains the hook and carries the offer. This is where the numbers live: capital, split,
cadence, price. Two sentences. The reader who got here is interested, so give them the facts.
---

## Body

Long copy works for interested buyers. Readership falls fast to 50 words, then barely moves to
500. Load it with specifics. Every adjective is a missing number.

State what the thing costs and what it does not include. Copy that disqualifies the wrong buyer
saves refunds, complaints and one-star reviews.
---

## Interface copy

Buttons start with a verb and say what happens. "Start your trial", not "Submit". The label
and the result must match: a button that says Publish produces a toast that says Published.

Errors say what broke and how to fix it, next to where it broke. No apologies.

Empty states point forward to the first useful action. Placeholders are examples, not labels.

Links describe their destination. "See the rules", not "click here".

Derive the capitalisation policy from the existing product and hold it everywhere.

**FAQ headings stay plain.** *Frequently asked questions*, not a clever section name. The
clever one is unindexable and the rich result is the whole reason the block exists.
Ship the accordion with FAQPage schema before building one URL per answer.
---

## Voice

The voice is derived, not assumed. Read it from the brand's own material (for campaigns,
`campaign-copy` section 2 writes it into `brand.md` as a voice card). Without a voice card,
derive a short one first:

1. Collect 3–5 pieces the brand rates: ads, landing pages, emails.
2. Measure them: sentence length, person ("you", "we"), formality, humour, punctuation
   habits (dashes, exclamation marks), capitalisation, how numbers are written.
3. Write the rules you see, each with an example from the sources, and get them approved.
4. Add one rule per rejected draft, in the reviewer's words.

Tics to look for in any draft, whatever the voice (these are checks, not house style):
adverbs and hedges doing no work, throat-clearing openers, the "Not X, it's Y" contrast
used as a reflex, lists that are always three items long, and every paragraph ending on a
punchline. Keep them only if the brand's own material uses them.

Specific beats clever. Clear beats both.

## Research

**Competitor headlines** tell you what not to write. Expect most of the category to be saying
one thing in different words. That is the opening.

**Voice of customer** gives vocabulary, never strategy. Mine reviews, tickets and calls for the
phrases buyers use. Decide separately what to lead with. The most repeated customer
phrase is often a hygiene factor.

**Reviews split by rating** are the best source in the pile. Promoters tell you what to keep.
Detractors tell you, in their words, the objection the page must survive.

**Keywords before the phrase.** Pull real terms from competitor URLs, titles and SERPs. Then
compose a line that carries one. Retro-fitting keywords to a finished headline produces a worse
headline. The title tag carries most of the ranking weight, so the h1 is usually free to sell.

**Competitor FAQs are the cheapest research in the pile.** Some firms publish one URL per
answer, so their whole question list harvests from a sitemap in one call. Expect it to
contain the operational blockers you invented your way past: what payment methods, how
many accounts, is the fee refunded, can I cancel, do I pay tax.
Objection-derived FAQs miss all of those, and those are the ones that stop a purchase.

**Say when a source failed.** If the SERP scrape was blocked, the set is grounded in what
competitors answer, which is a proxy for demand and not evidence of it. Write that down
next to the work rather than letting it pass as researched.
---

## Final pass

Run these over a finished draft, one at a time. Mixing them means catching none of them.

1. **Clarity.** Would a stranger understand this in one read?
2. **So what.** After every feature sentence, ask it. If there is no answer, cut the sentence.
3. **Prove it.** Every claim carries a number, a name, or a date. Unprovable claims come out.
4. **Specificity.** Replace each adjective with a fact.
5. **Voice.** One voice throughout. Headlines louder, body plainer, buttons active.
6. **Risk.** Name what the reader is committing to and what it costs. Hiding it raises refunds.

## Pre-flight

- Whose benefit, finished as "so you can…" without naming the company
- One audience
- Every claim has an owner after launch
- Vocabulary lane signed off by whoever owns the risk
- Headline leads, does not defend or gatekeep
- One idea, no price, no spec sheet
- Syntax not already owned by a rival
- Keyword list came before the phrase
- One primary action, repeated. Nothing outranks it above the fold

## Writing the document, not just the copy

**Decisions, not narrative.** A copy deck carries what is live, what is in reserve, and
what was rejected with the one-line reason. It does not carry the story of how you got
there. The history is worth exactly one line per rejected option — enough that nobody
re-proposes it, and nothing more.

**Every claim keeps its owner and its status.** Mark unverified facts in brackets and keep
the open questions on the front page, not buried. A reviewer needs to know which numbers
are real before they can judge the sentence around them.

**Alternatives travel beside the copy, not in a separate file.** Whoever rejects the live
line wants the next option in the same eyeline, along with why the others died.
---

## When it keeps getting rejected

Stop producing variants. Four rounds is a missing input, not a craft problem. Ask for one:

- a headline from any industry they rate
- the reaction they want in the first two seconds
- the specific reader they are writing to

More options from the same narrow pattern will keep failing.
